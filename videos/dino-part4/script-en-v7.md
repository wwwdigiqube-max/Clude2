## 0 · The River Screams
[DAY 88 AFTER · NIGHT]
In the middle of the night, the river screams.
A long, low howl, right outside, from the ice. Then a crack, like a gunshot, running away into the dark.
You're lying in a hole you dug in the snow under a rock on the bank, a few steps from the river.
Pip's heart is going fast against your neck.
The ice isn't breaking. (pause) You hope.
This morning, you still had a tunnel, a fire and a door.
Tonight you have a backpack, a spear, and a shell full of fire.
And a river that runs east, all the way to the sea.
If the map is right.

## 1 · No Walls
[DAY 88 AFTER]
You leave in the morning, as soon as the grey light comes.
The ice is flat and grey, like a road no one else uses. The burnt forest slides past on both banks.
You count your steps. A thousand. Two thousand. Ten thousand. Then you stop counting.
Every hour, you open the fire shell and blow into it, gently. The fungus glows orange, then dark again.
As long as it glows, you have fire.

[GRAPHIC: seen from above, the frozen river winding east through the dead forest; a small red dot: YOU; a dashed line to a grey sea at the edge · THE SEA: ? DAYS]
Nobody knows how long this winter will last. Months. Maybe years. Back at the tunnel there was just enough food to stay alive, and "just enough" doesn't last years. And your dad always said winters are kinder by the water.
On the book's map, the sea is one finger away.
You don't know how many days a finger is. You packed fish for two weeks, and hoped.

And out here, one thing matters more than how far you get.
In the tunnel, the night didn't matter. You had walls.
Out here, the night is what kills you. The cold drops, the wind rises, and you can't see the ice under your feet.
So before the light goes, you need a roof. Whatever you find. Wherever you are.
[RULE 6: BE UNDER A ROOF BEFORE DARK]

## 2 · The Ones Who Didn't Make It
At midday, you eat half a dried fish without stopping. It rode under your jacket all morning, so it's soft enough to bite. Half. That's the plan.
Then something dark lies across the ice ahead.
Near the tunnel, the hunters ate the dead until there were no hunters left. Out here, nothing was left to eat them.
A duck-billed dinosaur, as long as a bus. It must have died at the water's edge in the first weeks of the dark, and when the river froze, the ice closed around it. Now half of it is locked in the river.
Your stomach says: meat. A whole mountain of it.
But it died long before the ice came. Even in this cold, you can smell it. And out here, one fever is the end.
Rule four says: eat what eats the dead. Not the dead.

Pip's mate pokes her head out of your pocket. She sniffs the air, jumps down, and digs into a fold of the frozen skin.
Beetles. Hundreds of them, frozen solid, packed into the folds.
She eats like she's never eaten before. Pip is right behind her.
Nobody had to teach them rule four.
You wait. And you fill one pocket with beetles, for later.

In the afternoon, the bank rises on your left, and from the snow on top, a huge head looks down at you.
Three horns. A huge bony frill. A Triceratops, lying on its side where it fell, frozen whole, a drift of grey snow along its back and frost in the corner of its eye.
You climb up and put your hand on the horn above its eye. It's taller than you.
"Every big animal you see will die." You've known that since the first day.
Knowing it is different from seeing it.
(pause)
You stay too long.
When you climb down, the grey is already turning dark.

And you haven't gathered any wood.
The bank here is steep. Bare clay and stones. No drift, no hollow, nothing.
The wind picks up. You walk faster. Then you run, slipping, the fire shell knocking against your hip.
Then you see it: a shelf of rock sticking out of the bank. The wind blows the snow up over the top of it, and behind the rock, out of the wind, the snow falls and piles up, deep and soft.
You dig in from the side, right under the rock. The stone is your back wall. The snow is your roof.
Then, in the last grey minutes, you drag dead branches down from the burnt forest above. Black and dry inside. As many as you can carry.

A house made of snow. Snow is mostly air, and trapped air is the best blanket there is.
[CARD: REAL / a snow cave can be 20°C warmer than the air outside · Arctic peoples have built snow shelters for thousands of years]

At the mouth of the den, you lift the clay lid of the fire shell, lay the glowing fungus in a pinch of the fern fluff you packed, and blow. A small fire. Outside the den, never inside: in a closed space, smoke kills quietly. You poke an air hole through the roof with the spear anyway.
Pip and his mate crawl into your collar.
The last grey light goes out.
You made it. Just.

Before you sleep, you count. Every night, from now on.
[LIST: THE JOURNEY · dried fish: 13½ · dried grubs: two handfuls · beetles: one pocket · water: 1 shell · fire: alive · feet: sore]
Fourteen fish when you left. Half a fish a day on the move, a whole one every second night. Tonight is a whole one.
It's frozen as hard as wood. You thaw it on a stone hot from the fire, the way you cooked your first catch through the ice. A chunk of river ice melts in the water shell beside it. River ice is cleaner than the ashy snow. And you never eat ice raw. It steals your heat.
A fresh piece of fungus and a glowing coal go into the fire shell. Tomorrow's fire, packed.
Then your boots come off. You rub your feet until they hurt. Hurting is good. Numb is bad.
And the last thing, every night: "Night, Mom."

And for a while, it works.
Then the river starts to scream.

[CARD: THE ICE IS SINGING / it's getting thicker, not breaking]
The ice isn't breaking. It's getting thicker.
When the night gets colder, the ice shrinks and splits, and the cracks run for miles. It sounds like the world is breaking.
But ice that sings is ice that's getting stronger.
By morning, you're not afraid of the sound anymore. (pause)
Almost.

## 3 · Meanwhile
[NOW · DAY 98 MISSING]
Sixty-six million years later, it's the same night. Your mom still hasn't gone home.
She sits in the back room of the museum, across the table from the old woman, the bone between them.
"Where exactly?" she asks.
The old woman unfolds an old field map, soft as cloth, covered in notes. Your dad's handwriting.
"Daniel's old dig site. A river ran through there, sixty-six million years ago." Her finger touches one spot. "This is where we found the rex. The two of us. Thirty years ago."
Her finger moves the width of a fingernail. "And this is where I found the bone. Fifty steps away. The same riverbank."
Your mom thinks about her dream. A small fire under the roots of a huge black tree.
"Was there a tree?"
The old woman looks at her for a long time.
"Animals dig homes in riverbanks," she says at last. "Under the roots, where the bank holds. When the river floods, the holes fill with mud. Sometimes, millions of years later, we find them. Turned to stone."
She doesn't say anything else. She doesn't have to.

[NOW · DAY 99 MISSING]
In the morning, your two best friends come to the museum, the way they have every week since you disappeared. They still stand where you filmed your video.
This time, your mom is waiting for them. She asks to see it.
You, laughing at the camera, a giant skeleton behind you.
"If they ever sent me back to the dinosaurs? I'd survive. Easy."
Your mom watches it four times.
The skeleton behind you is the one your dad dug out of the ground. The one you sat in front of on his shoulders, when you were five.
And sixty-six million years ago, it died at your door.
(pause)
That evening, four people get into one car. A mother. An old woman with your dad's field map on her knees. And your two best friends, who won't take no for an answer.
They drive through the night, to North Dakota. To your dad's old dig site.

## 4 · Black Ice
[DAY 89 AFTER]
In the morning, your legs feel like someone else's. The backpack feels heavier, even though it's lighter by one and a half fish.
You leave at first light anyway. The earlier you start, the more daylight you have to find a roof.

By midday, the river changes. The banks close in, steep and high, and the river squeezes between them.
Here the ice isn't white. It's dark. And under it, you can see the water moving. Fast.
Where a river runs fast, it freezes thin.
You tap the ice ahead with the end of the spear.
On the third tap, a white line shoots out from the spear. Then another.
You don't breathe. You step back, one slow foot at a time, until the ice under you is white again.

[CARD: DARK ICE + FAST WATER = THIN ICE / where the current runs fast, the river can't freeze thick]

So you leave the river. You climb the bank and walk through the dead forest, around the narrows.
It's slow. Fallen trunks, ash up to your knees, every step twice as hard as on the ice.
And it's here that you find them.
A whole herd of duck-billed dinosaurs, lying close together in a hollow between the trees. Not a fight. No wounds. They just lay down together, out of the wind, and never got up.
Now they're long humps of grey snow. Here a tail sticks out, there a flat beak, frozen hard. Where something small has been digging, a few ribs show through, like the frames of an upturned boat.
You walk through them without speaking.

Then something moves on one of the skulls.
A bird. Small and brown, no bigger than your hand, with a short, sharp beak. It's pecking at the frozen hide. At something in the folds.
It looks at you. You look at it.
It's the first bird you've seen since the sky went dark.
Then it flies away, low, toward the east.
(pause)
So some of them really did make it.

By the time you're back on the white ice past the narrows, the light is already going grey.
You lost half a day.
Rule six. You stop early. Wood first, then the den. This time, you're under your roof with light to spare.

## 5 · The Shell
[DAY 91 AFTER]
On the fourth day, the wind comes from the east, straight into your face, full of grey snow. You walk bent over, eyes half shut, counting nothing.
You forget to open the fire shell. Once. Twice.
At noon, you remember. You lift the clay lid.
Grey ash. No glow.
(pause)
You don't move. Then you tip it, slowly, and at the very bottom of the ash there's one red dot. Smaller than a seed.
You turn your back to the wind and kneel over it like you're hiding it from the whole world.
Fluff. You pull a tuft of fern fluff out of the wrap on your arm, the fluff that's kept you warm since you left, and lay it on the red dot. You blow. Not hard. Like blowing on a dandelion.
Nothing.
Again.
A thread of smoke.
Again.
The fluff turns orange at one edge, then the whole tuft, and you feed it a curl of fungus, and then another, and the shell is warm in your hands again.
You sit in the snow for a long time, holding it.
This fire came from a burning forest on the last day of the dinosaurs. You've carried it for ninety-one days. It nearly died because you were tired.
From then on, you open the shell every hour. Even when you're tired. Especially then.
[CARD: REAL / tinder fungus can smoulder for hours · people carried fire like this for thousands of years]

## 6 · The Roots
[NOW · DAY 100 MISSING]
Sixty-six million years later, a car stops at the end of a frozen dirt track, in the North Dakota badlands.
Grey hills under old snow, cut into stripes by wind and rain. In one of those stripes, a thin pale line runs through the rock: the layer the asteroid left behind.
The old woman walks ahead, the way she's walked these hills for forty years.
"Wind and rain peel these hills back a little every year," she says. "Everything that was buried here comes back out, one storm at a time. That's how we find anything."
At the foot of a low cliff, she stops at a white post with a number on it. "The rex," she says. "Your dad found the first tooth. Right here."
Fifty steps along the same dark stripe of old river clay, a small square of earth with string around it. "The bone."
Your friends look from one to the other. Everything happened on one small piece of riverbank.
Your mom isn't listening.
She's standing back at the white post, looking up at the cliff above it.
Halfway up the rock, something dark and twisted sticks out of the stone. Thick, knotted shapes, spreading down and outward. Roots. A whole tree's worth of roots, turned to stone.
A small fire under the roots of a huge black tree.
She's seen these roots before.
In a dream.

## 7 · The Same Day
[DAY 93–100 AFTER]
By the sixth day, the days stop being days.
They turn into one day, over and over. Grey light. Ice. Half a fish. Wood. Den. Fire. Count. Sleep. The river screams. Grey light.
You stop looking at the dead forest. You only notice what changes.

## 8 · Back
[DAY 101 AFTER]
At noon you sit down on the ice and open the backpack.
One and a half fish. A handful of grubs. A pocket of beetles.
You do the sum you've been not doing for three days.
You packed for two weeks. It's been thirteen days. And on the map, you're still not there.
Behind you, thirteen days back, there's a tunnel. You know every root in its ceiling.
You stand up. You turn around. West.
And you walk ten steps back the way you came.
(pause)
Then you stop.
Because you know what's back there. Just enough. A grub log, a crack in the ice, a fish on a good day. Just enough to stay alive for a while.
And nobody knows how long this winter will last. Months. Maybe years.
"The sea is slow. It takes forever to cool down. That's why winters are kinder by the water." Your dad, at the window, watching the snow. That's why you came. Nothing has changed.
Above you, a flock of small brown birds goes over, low, all in the same direction.
East.
They don't have a map. They don't need one.
You turn around again.

[DAY 102 AFTER]
Beetles at noon. One fish at night, the second to last.
[DAY 103 AFTER]
The last half fish. You don't count anything anymore. You just walk.
And in the evening, at the mouth of the den, the wind changes.
It smells different.
You lick your lips.
Salt.

## 9 · The Sea Is Slow
[DAY 104 AFTER]
In the morning, the light is different. Still grey. But a lighter grey, like paper held up to a lamp.
The ice under your feet gets thinner, and greyer. You test every step with the spear. Then it isn't ice anymore. It's slush. Then water, moving, steaming in the cold air.
You climb the last bank.
And there it is.
Flat and grey and endless, all the way to the edge of the world. Not frozen. Waves, small and slow, rolling up onto a beach of dark sand. Steam rising off it, like breath.
"The sea is slow. It takes forever to cool down." Your dad was right.
A whole sea holds so much heat that months of winter can't freeze it. While the land froze, the sea stayed warm. The first water in a hundred days that isn't ice.
You walk down to the water. You take off your boots. You put your feet in.
It hurts. Hurting is good.
Pip climbs out onto your shoulder and stares at it.
Then you see something at your feet, in the dark sand, where the warm water meets the shore.
Something green.
A fern. A tiny curl of a fern, no bigger than your thumbnail. The first green thing you've seen since the last green fern, months ago.
[CARD: REAL / after the impact, ferns were the first plants to come back · scientists call it the fern spike]
(pause)
You don't cry. You're too tired to cry.
You just sit down on the sand.
At the waterline, the sand is full of shells. Some of them are closed. Alive.
[CARD: REAL / clams and other shellfish survived the extinction better than most animals]
Food.
And you say it out loud. "Night, Mom." Even though it's morning.

[NOW · DAY 114 MISSING]
Sixty-six million years later, your mom and the old woman have spent two weeks in a motel in the nearest town. Your friends come out every weekend.
The old woman won't let anyone dig into the cliff. Not without a permit, not in the frozen ground. So they brush, and look, and wait. Under the stone roots, the rock is hidden behind a crust of frozen mud.
On Friday night, a warm wind comes down from the west. By Saturday morning, the snow on the hills has melted, and the meltwater has run down the cliff face all night, washing it clean.
One of your friends stops. Right on the pale stripe, under the stone roots, a line in the rock. Straight. Flat. Too flat for a rock.
Like a slab of stone. Like a door.

[CTA: If you reached the end of the world, what's the first thing you'd do?]
Stay small. Stay alive.
