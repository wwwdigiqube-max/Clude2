# Season 1 · Part 2: The Asteroid Hit 40 Days Ago. You Have 7 Days of Food. (script EN v5, after story-audit round 2)

> Second person, present tense, short lines, same voice as Part 1.
> `[DAY n AFTER]` = corner tag (days after the impact) · `[NOW · DAY n MISSING]` = present-day card (days since he vanished = days after impact + 10) · `(pause)` = held beat.
> New in v2:
> - His father: a paleontologist who died when he was seven. Through memories, not genes.
> - The locked room (how his mom found out).
> - "40 days after the impact = 50 days here" said clearly.
> - More real survival tricks (a grub log, the sand pit for water, keeping the fire).

## 0 · Seven Fish

[DAY 49 AFTER]
The last fish is gone. Two days ago.
You scrape something white off the wall of your tunnel and hold it up to the fire.
It's soft. It smells like the forest after rain.
Pip sniffs it. And he's watching you.
Nine days ago, you had seven fish.
(pause)
You have no food left. And nobody is coming.
At least, not for 66 million years.

## 1 · The Last of Them

[DAY 40 AFTER]
Forty days since the impact. Fifty since you woke up in this world.
Seven fish lie on the shelf you cut into the clay. One a day. You've done the math. Seven days.
Outside, the sky is the colour of wet ash. Some mornings, the ash is snow.

You remember the first weeks after the impact.
A few dinosaurs made it through the fire too. A duck-bill, so thin you could count its ribs, chewing on burnt branches.
But with no sun, nothing green grows. Big animals need huge amounts of food every day. For a while, the hunters ate the dead. Then the dead ran out too.
It wasn't only the fire that killed the dinosaurs. It was the hunger after it.
And the same hunger is now waiting at your door.

Back home, on your mom's nightstand, there's a photo: you, five years old, on your dad's shoulders, in front of that same T. rex skeleton.
You haven't let yourself think about him since you got here. Tonight you do.
When you were small, your dad used to put you to bed under his dinosaur posters.
He dug up fossils for a living. He'd come home with dust in his hair and a story about a bone.
He died when you were seven. The posters stayed.
And every night, you lay under them and wished, just once, to see a real one.
(pause)
You got your wish.
You saw them.
And you watched the last of them die.

## 2 · Meanwhile

[NOW · DAY 50 MISSING]
Back home, sixty-six million years from now, it's day fifty.
On the first morning, your mom knocked to wake you for school. No answer.
Your door was locked from the inside. A neighbour had to break it open.
The bed was empty. The window was shut.
The police still can't explain it.
There's a poster on every lamp post in town. Your face. MISSING.
Your mom hasn't moved a thing in your room. Your dad's dinosaur posters. The charger, still plugged into the wall.

Your two best friends heard it at school, the morning you didn't show up.
The first place they looked was the museum. When something was wrong, you always went to see the T. rex. The one your dad used to take you to.
You weren't there.
They still go back, every week. They stand where you filmed your video.
"I'd survive. Easy."
Nobody laughs anymore.

## 3 · Eat What Eats the Dead

[DAY 47 AFTER]
Back in the tunnel, the last fish is gone.
One day with nothing. Then two.
Your hands start to shake.
Water isn't the problem. You still dig a pit in the sand by the river, upstream, like on day three, and the grey water seeps in clearer.
Fish are. In this cold, the fish have gone deep, where your hands can't reach.

But Pip isn't starving. Before the fire, he hid seeds under the roots, the way small animals do. He's lived on those, and on bugs.
His stash is mouse-sized. A handful of seeds wouldn't last you an hour.
On day forty-nine, starving, you finally follow him.
He walks past the dead plants and goes straight for a rotten log.
He digs. And he comes out with a fat white grub.
Then another.
The forest is dead. But dead things are food, for something.

So you dig next to Pip. Grubs. Beetles. And on the tunnel walls, that soft white fungus.
You don't know which fungus is safe. Pip sniffs every kind. He eats the white. He won't touch the orange.
It's a terrible rule. Animals can eat things that would kill a person. But you're out of rules.
You eat one tiny piece of the white, and you wait a whole day. Nothing happens.
Even that proves nothing. Some deadly fungi take days to kill you. You got lucky.
So the white becomes food. And the orange, you never go near.

Then you get smart.
You drag wet, rotting logs right next to your door, and pile dead leaves on top.
Every few days, you roll one over. There are always grubs underneath.
For the first time since the impact, you have a farm.
Scientists think that's how so many small animals made it: everything that needed fresh plants starved, but the things that lived on the dead kept going.
Rule four: eat what eats the dead.
[LIST: FOOD: grub log, white fungus · WATER: sand pit · FIRE: dry wood stacked inside]

## 4 · A Message for Mom

[DAY 52 AFTER]
With food in your stomach, you can think again.
And the thought that comes back, every night, is your mom.
She doesn't know where you are. She never will.
You can't go back. You've known it for weeks. Tonight, you say it out loud.
"I can't go back."
Pip looks up at you.

So you stop waiting to wake up. What's coming is colder and longer than the impact. You'll need a plan.
Your pen is dry. But the fire leaves charcoal.
You pick up a black stick and write on the tunnel wall, like you wrote in the book on night one.
What you lost. What you have. What you still need.
[LIST: WINTER PLAN · FOOD THAT KEEPS ☐ · WATER WHEN THE RIVER FREEZES ☐ · FIREWOOD ☐ · A DEEPER SHELTER ?]

Your dad used to say: "Every bone is a message from someone who was here."
(pause)
Maybe you can't go home. But maybe a message can.
Almost nothing ever becomes a fossil. One bone in many millions. But you know how the lucky ones start: buried fast, deep in mud, like the T. rex at your door.
And you have a bone. The one you've been digging with since day four.
It's the only shovel you have. You'll bury it anyway.
So you take a sharp stone, and you start to write on it.
It takes three nights. Your fingers bleed. Your right arm still aches where the claw went in. You go over every letter again and again, deep enough to last forever.
Three words.
The last two are almost what you said in front of that skeleton, laughing. Except this time, they're true.
MOM. I SURVIVED.

## 5 · The River at Dawn

Now it has to be buried. Deep. In mud. Where the river floods.
The mud at your door is frozen hard as rock. The only soft clay left is down at the crocodile's pool.
The river is still running, grey and cold, with ice along the edges in the mornings.
And the river has survivors of its own.
A crocodile. Maybe the same one from day three. Crocodiles made it through the end of the world.
And beside it, a champsosaur: a long, thin snout, built for catching fish. It made it through too.
Crocodiles are cold-blooded. In this cold, they barely move. That's how they survived: they almost stop. Almost.
They're slowest at dawn, before the day warms them. So you go at first light.
You crawl down the bank with the bone on your back.
At the water's edge, where the river floods every spring, you push the bone deep under the clay, as deep as your arm can reach.
Something moves in the water.
Pip squeaks. Once. Loud.
A dark shape drifts toward you, slow as a log.
You throw yourself backwards, and the crocodile's jaws close, lazily, where your hand just was.
(pause)
Slow. But not slow enough to be safe.
The bone is under the clay. You push the last handful over it and run.

Back in the tunnel, you sit with your back against the stone door, shaking.
It's done. Whatever happens to you now, something of you will last.

## 6 · Not Alone

A few days later, Pip doesn't come back at night.
You wait. One night. Two.
[DAY 60 AFTER]
On the third morning, he's at the entrance.
And he's not alone.
A second little face. Smaller. The same brown fur. The same black eyes.
She survived the dark too, in some other hole, under some other tree.
Pip isn't the last of his kind.
(pause)
You're happy for him. You really are.
But that night, the tunnel feels bigger than it ever has.

The cold is getting worse. One morning, the sand pit is frozen solid.
So you work down the list on the wall. You dry grubs hard on a flat stone by the fire, the way you dried fish. They'll keep for weeks.
You bring snow inside, melt it by the fire, and pour it through sand and charcoal, because this snow is full of ash.
One tick. Then another.
But one line on the wall still has a question mark.
Your tunnel was built to survive the impact. Not a winter.
You'll need somewhere deeper. Warmer.
But that's a problem for another day.
Tonight, you scratch a new line on the wall.
Friends: one.
You stop. And you add:
…and one more.

## 7 · Sixty-Six Million Years Later

[NOW · DAY 70 MISSING]
North Dakota. Your dad's old dig site. A hot summer afternoon.
An old paleontologist who used to dig here with your dad brushes the dust off the shoulder blade of a duck-billed dinosaur.
She stops.
There are marks on the bone. Not teeth marks. Too straight. Too deep.
She brushes again.
They're not scratches.
They're letters.
(pause)
If you could leave one message for 66 million years… what would you write on the bone?
Tell me in the comments.

Stay small. Stay alive.
